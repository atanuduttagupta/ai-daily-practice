First, what is a metric? A metric is simply a measurement that helps us
judge a model. Clustering has no single perfect score because different
metrics ask different questions. Some ask whether points are close to
their own cluster, some ask whether clusters are separated, some compare
the answer with known labels, and some balance model fit against model
complexity.

What is inertia? (K-Means) Easy meaning: Imagine every cluster has a
centre point. Inertia adds up how far every data point is from the
centre of the cluster it was assigned to. The distances are squared
before they are added.

How to read it: - Lower inertia means points are, on average, packed
more closely around their assigned centroids. - Inertia is never
negative. - Inertia usually becomes smaller when you increase k, because
more centres give points more nearby places to attach to. - Therefore,
the lowest inertia alone does NOT tell us the best number of clusters.

Simple example: If k=2 gives inertia 1,000 and k=3 gives inertia 650,
the k=3 model fits the training points more tightly. But we still need
to ask whether the extra cluster is meaningful.

Interview answer: “Inertia is the total within-cluster sum of squared
distances from observations to their assigned K-Means centroids. Lower
is tighter, but it naturally decreases as k increases.”

What is the Elbow Method? Easy meaning: Try several values of k, such as
1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6. Draw a graph with k on the horizontal axis and
inertia on the vertical axis. At first, adding a cluster may improve the
fit a lot. Later, the improvement may become small. The bend where the
improvement starts slowing down looks like an elbow.

How to read it: Choose a k near the bend if there is a clear bend. It is
a visual clue, not a mathematical proof. Some datasets do not have a
clear elbow.

What is Silhouette Score? (K-Means or other clusterings) Easy meaning:
Silhouette asks two questions about each point: 1. Is this point close
to the other points in its own cluster? 2. Is it far away from the
nearest other cluster?

It combines those two answers into one score for each point, then
averages the scores.

The simple terms: - a = average distance from this point to other points
in its own cluster. Think: “How far am I from my own group?” - b = the
smallest average distance from this point to points in any other
cluster. Think: “How far am I from the closest competing group?” -
silhouette = (b - a) / max(a, b)

How to read the score: - Close to +1: good placement. The point is much
closer to its own group than to another. - Around 0: the point is near a
boundary between groups, or groups overlap. - Below 0: the point may be
closer to another cluster than to the one it was assigned to.

The overall silhouette score is usually the average across all points: -
Higher is generally better for compact, well-separated clusters. - The
theoretical range is -1 to +1. - A high score does not automatically
mean the clusters are useful for the business problem. - Silhouette can
favour fewer, compact, well-separated clusters and may not agree with
other measures.

Tiny example: Suppose a point’s average distance to its own cluster is
a=2 and its average distance to the nearest other cluster is b=6. Its
silhouette is (6-2)/6 = 0.67, which is a reasonably good placement.

Interview answer: “Silhouette measures how well each point fits its own
cluster compared with the nearest alternative cluster. It ranges from -1
to +1; higher is generally better.”

What is ARI — Adjusted Rand Index? Easy meaning: ARI checks how closely
our clustering result matches a set of correct/reference group labels,
when those labels are available. It compares pairs of data points: - Did
the reference labels put these two points in the same group? - Did our
clustering also put them in the same group? It checks agreement across
pairs, and adjusts the result to account for agreement that could happen
by chance.

A simple analogy: A teacher has the correct grouping of students. You
group the same students without seeing the answer. ARI measures how
closely your grouping matches the teacher’s grouping, while allowing for
some agreement that could happen randomly.

How to read ARI: - +1 means the two groupings match perfectly (cluster
names/order do not matter). - Around 0 means the agreement is about what
would be expected by chance. - A negative value means the agreement is
worse than chance under the ARI adjustment. - It is an external metric:
it needs reference/true labels. - In real unsupervised problems, true
labels are often not available. We had them in this project because we
generated the synthetic data and therefore knew the original groups.

Important: ARI does not tell us whether clusters are compact or well
separated geometrically. It tells us whether the predicted grouping
agrees with the reference grouping.

Project example: The README reports K-Means ARI = 0.718 and GMM ARI =
0.942. In this particular synthetic experiment, GMM’s grouping matched
the generated reference groups more closely. These are project-run
results, not universal performance guarantees.

Interview answer: “ARI compares a predicted clustering with known
reference labels by measuring pairwise agreement and correcting for
chance. One is a perfect match, around zero is chance-level, and it
requires reference labels.”

What is BIC — Bayesian Information Criterion? (GMM) Easy meaning: BIC
helps us compare candidate statistical models while considering two
things: 1. How well does the model explain the observed data? 2. How
complicated is the model?

A model can fit the data better simply by adding more components and
parameters. BIC applies a penalty for complexity so that adding
components is not rewarded for fit alone.

Think of it like choosing a school project: A very complicated project
may fit every detail, but it takes more effort and may be overfitting.
BIC balances “how well it explains the data” against “how complicated it
is.”

How to read BIC: - When comparing candidate models fitted to the same
dataset, using comparable likelihood assumptions, a lower BIC is
preferred. - BIC has no universal good/bad cutoff. The raw number is
meaningful mainly in comparison with other candidate models on the same
data. - BIC is not a percentage and is not restricted to 0–1. - It
penalizes model complexity; adding more components does not
automatically make BIC better.

Formula: BIC = -2 × log(likelihood) + p × log(n) where: - likelihood =
how well the fitted model explains the observed data (higher is
better); - log = natural logarithm; - p = number of estimated model
parameters; - n = number of observations.

The first term rewards a model that explains the data well. The second
term penalizes a model with many parameters.

Project example: The README reports that GMM BIC was lowest at k=3 among
the tested values k=2 through 6. That supported choosing three Gaussian
components for this synthetic dataset.

Interview answer: “BIC compares model fit while penalizing complexity.
For candidate GMMs fitted to the same data, lower BIC is generally
preferred; it helps avoid adding unnecessary components.”

What are AIC and BIC? How are they different? Both compare statistical
models by balancing fit and complexity. - AIC = -2 × log(likelihood) +
2p - BIC = -2 × log(likelihood) + p × log(n) Here p is the number of
estimated parameters and n is the number of observations. BIC’s penalty
grows with the sample size and is often stronger than AIC’s for
sufficiently large datasets. They can select different models; neither
is a universal truth.

What is log-likelihood? Easy meaning: It measures how well a probability
model says the observed data could have occurred. A higher
log-likelihood (less negative, when values are negative) means the model
assigns more probability density to the observed data. But a more
complicated model can often increase likelihood, which is why AIC and
BIC add complexity penalties.

What is GMM membership probability / responsibility? Easy meaning: For
each data point, GMM estimates how likely each Gaussian component is to
have generated that point, given the fitted model. For example, [0.70,
0.25, 0.05] means: - Component 1: 70% of the model’s membership
probability - Component 2: 25% - Component 3: 5% The values for one
point add up to 1. They are model-based probabilities, not guaranteed
real-world truths. A point with [0.36, 0.34, 0.30] is much more
ambiguous than a point with [0.95, 0.03, 0.02].

What does maximum membership probability below 0.9 mean? It means the
largest of the component probabilities for that point is below 90%. The
model does not assign that point very strongly to any one component. The
0.9 threshold is a chosen rule for this project, not a universal
definition of uncertainty.

Quick metric comparison — what question does each answer?

Metric Plain-English question Needs true labels? Inertia Are points
close to their assigned centroids? No Silhouette Is each point closer to
its own group than No to the nearest other group? ARI Does my grouping
match the known/reference Yes grouping, beyond chance agreement? BIC
Which GMM balances data fit and model No complexity better? AIC Which
candidate model balances fit and No complexity under AIC’s penalty?

Why can metrics disagree? They are answering different questions: -
Silhouette judges geometric closeness and separation. - ARI checks
agreement with known/reference labels. - BIC compares probabilistic
model fit while penalizing complexity. - Inertia measures closeness to
K-Means centroids and always tends to fall as k grows.

Your project demonstrates this clearly: - K-Means: ARI 0.718; silhouette
0.614. - GMM: ARI 0.942; silhouette 0.612. - For candidate k, silhouette
preferred k=2 (0.659), while GMM BIC was lowest at k=3. This is not a
contradiction. The measures evaluate different properties. Interpret
them together with plots, stability and the actual purpose of
clustering.

A practical rule for interviews: Never say “the highest metric always
wins” without naming the metric and explaining what it measures. Say
which question the metric answers, whether it needs labels, and what its
limitations are.

K-Means & GMM | Interview Study Guide Page 1  1. Clustering foundations
What is clustering? Clustering is an unsupervised learning task that
groups observations so that points within a group are more similar to
one another than to points in other groups, under a chosen
representation and similarity measure.

Why is clustering unsupervised? The model is not trained with a target
label for every observation. It discovers structure from input features.
Labels may be available later for evaluation, but they are not used to
fit the clustering model.

What are common applications? Customer segmentation, document and
embedding grouping, image segmentation, product grouping, data
exploration, near-duplicate detection and anomaly exploration.

How does clustering differ from classification? Classification is
supervised and predicts known labels learned from labelled examples.
Clustering is unsupervised and discovers groups. A cluster ID is not
automatically a meaningful business category.

What does a cluster represent? A group coherent under the chosen
features, scaling, distance measure and algorithm. It is not necessarily
a naturally occurring or objectively correct category; usefulness
depends on the application.

What is hard versus soft clustering? Hard clustering assigns each point
to one cluster. Soft clustering gives a degree or probability of
membership in each cluster. K-Means is hard; a GMM can provide soft
membership probabilities.

Do cluster numbers have inherent meaning? No. IDs such as 0, 1 and 2 are
arbitrary. Different runs can find equivalent partitions but number them
differently. Compare memberships or cluster profiles, not raw IDs.

2.  K-Means algorithm and limitations Explain K-Means. Choose k and
    initialize k centroids. Assign each observation to its nearest
    centroid. Recalculate each centroid as the mean of its assigned
    observations. Repeat assignment and update until convergence or a
    stopping limit.

What objective does K-Means minimize? Within-cluster sum of squared
distances, called inertia: Σⱼ Σₓ∈Cⱼ ||x − μⱼ||². Here Cⱼ is a cluster
and μⱼ its centroid. Lower training inertia means points are closer to
their assigned centroids, but does not prove the clustering is useful.

Why is the centroid the mean? For squared Euclidean distance, the
arithmetic mean minimizes the sum of squared distances from the
observations in a cluster. That is why the update step uses the mean.

What is the role of k? It is the number of clusters requested. Standard
K-Means does not infer k automatically; the practitioner must select it
or compare candidate values.

What shapes does K-Means handle best? It tends to work best for compact,
roughly spherical or convex clusters with broadly comparable scale and
density under Euclidean distance. It can struggle with elongated,
overlapping, non-convex or very unequal groups.

Why is feature scaling important? K-Means commonly uses Euclidean
distance. Features with larger numeric ranges can dominate distance
calculations. Scaling can prevent units or magnitudes from overwhelming
other features.

What is K-Means++? An initialization method that spreads initial
centroids out rather than choosing them completely at random. It often
improves convergence and solution quality but does not guarantee the
global optimum.

K-Means & GMM | Interview Study Guide Page 2  Why use n_init greater
than one? Different initial centroids can lead to different local
solutions. Multiple initializations reduce sensitivity to a poor
starting point. Your project uses n_init=10 for K-Means.

Does K-Means find the global optimum? Not necessarily. Its
assignment/update procedure converges to a local optimum or stationary
solution, not guaranteed to the globally best partition.

What happens if k is too small or too large? Too small can merge
distinct groups. Too large can fragment groups or model noise as
separate clusters. Inertia generally decreases as k increases, so it
cannot select k by itself.

How does K-Means handle outliers? It can be sensitive because centroids
are means and the objective squares distances. Extreme observations can
pull centroids away from dense regions. Consider robust preprocessing or
another method.

What are K-Means’ advantages? It is simple, efficient for many datasets,
easy to implement and interpret, and provides useful centroids. It is a
good baseline.

What are its limitations? It requires k, is sensitive to scaling and
initialization, can be affected by outliers, favours compact Euclidean
clusters and does not provide membership probabilities.

3.  Gaussian Mixture Models and EM What is a Gaussian Mixture Model? A
    GMM represents a data distribution as a weighted sum of k Gaussian
    components. Each component has a mean, covariance matrix and mixture
    weight. A point can have a probability of belonging to each
    component.

What are the parameters of a GMM? Each component has a mean vector μₖ,
covariance matrix Σₖ and mixing coefficient πₖ. Weights are non-negative
and sum to one.

Write the GMM density. p(x) = Σₖ πₖ (x | μₖ, Σₖ). Each Gaussian
contributes a density weighted by its mixture coefficient.

What is Expectation-Maximization? EM iteratively estimates parameters
when the model includes latent or unobserved variables. For a GMM, the
hidden variable is component membership. EM alternates between
estimating memberships and updating component parameters.

Explain the E-step. The E-step computes each responsibility rᵢₖ: the
posterior probability that component k generated observation i, given
current parameters. Responsibilities for a point sum to one.

Explain the M-step. The M-step updates mixture weights, means and
covariance matrices using responsibilities as fractional membership
weights.

Why is GMM soft clustering? Because predict_proba returns a probability
distribution over components for each observation. For example, a point
might have [0.70, 0.25, 0.05] membership probabilities.

How can GMM model elliptical clusters? A component’s covariance matrix
controls spread, orientation and correlations between dimensions. Full
covariance can represent a tilted ellipse in 2-D.

What does covariance_type control? full: each component has its own
unrestricted covariance. tied: all components share one full covariance.
diag: each has a diagonal covariance. spherical: each has one variance
value shared across dimensions.

predict versus predict_proba?

K-Means & GMM | Interview Study Guide Page 3  predict returns the most
likely component label. predict_proba returns the full posterior
probability vector over components.

Can GMM converge to a local optimum? Yes. EM can converge to a local
optimum or degenerate solution. Initialization, covariance constraints,
regularization and multiple initializations matter.

What is reg_covar? A non-negative value added to the covariance diagonal
to help keep covariance matrices positive definite and reduce numerical
instability.

What are GMM’s strengths? It provides probabilistic memberships and can
model overlapping groups and a wider range of cluster shapes, sizes and
orientations.

What are GMM’s limitations? It assumes a Gaussian-mixture
representation, requires model selection for component count, can be
initialization-sensitive and may be more computationally demanding than
K-Means.

When prefer GMM over K-Means? When clusters overlap, are elliptical or
have different covariance structures, or membership uncertainty is
useful. Validate that a Gaussian-mixture assumption is reasonable.

4.  Choosing the number of clusters Why is choosing k difficult? There
    may be no single objectively correct number. Criteria measure
    different aspects of fit, separation and complexity. The choice
    should also make sense for the downstream task.

What is the elbow method? Fit K-Means for several k values and plot
inertia. Look for the point where additional clusters produce a smaller
improvement. The elbow can be subjective or absent.

Why does inertia decrease as k increases? More centroids give the model
more flexibility to assign points closer to a centroid. With one
centroid per observation, inertia can reach zero. Complexity must be
considered.

What is silhouette score? For a point, let a be its average distance to
points in its own cluster and b the lowest average distance to points in
another cluster. Its score is (b − a) / max(a,b), ranging from -1 to 1.
Higher generally indicates better cohesion and separation.

What does a negative silhouette suggest? The point may be closer on
average to another cluster than to its assigned cluster. It can indicate
a questionable assignment or overlap.

What are silhouette’s limitations? It favours compact, well-separated
clusters, can under-reward complex structures, can be expensive on large
data and depends on representation and distance metric.

What are AIC and BIC? Information criteria balance model fit with model
complexity. For GMM selection they use likelihood and penalize the
number of estimated parameters. Lower is preferred among comparable
models fitted to the same data.

What is the BIC formula? A common form is BIC = −2 log(L) + p log(n),
where L is maximized likelihood, p the number of estimated parameters
and n the number of observations. Lower BIC is preferred.

Why can silhouette and BIC disagree? They optimize different criteria.
Silhouette measures geometric cohesion and separation; BIC evaluates
probabilistic fit with a complexity penalty. Disagreement is not
necessarily an error.

How do you select k in practice?

metrics, inspect plots and cluster sizes, assess stability across seeds
or samples, and determine whether groups are useful. Do not rely on one
metric.

5.  Evaluation metrics What is Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)? ARI compares
    two partitions, such as predicted clusters and known ground-truth
    labels, while adjusting for agreement expected by chance. It is 1
    for identical partitions, around 0 for chance-level agreement, and
    can be negative.

Does ARI require true labels? Yes. It is an external metric. In real
unlabeled clustering tasks, true labels may not exist, so ARI cannot be
computed directly.

Can ARI compare partitions with different numeric cluster IDs? Yes. ARI
is invariant to permutations of cluster identifiers and compares
pairwise grouping relationships.

Silhouette versus ARI? Silhouette is internal: it uses feature distances
and predicted clusters without ground-truth labels. ARI is external: it
measures agreement with a reference partition.

Why can two models have similar silhouette but different ARI? Silhouette
measures geometry, not whether groups match the known semantic or
generated labels. Different partitions can have similar geometry scores
but different agreement with truth.

Should the highest metric always win? No. Metrics are proxies and encode
assumptions. Consider stability, interpretability, domain usefulness,
downstream performance and error costs.

6.  Practical considerations Why preprocess before clustering? Inspect
    data quality, missing values, outliers, units, feature relevance and
    representation. Encode categorical variables appropriately and scale
    where model assumptions require it.

Should every feature be standardized? No. Scaling is often important for
Euclidean K-Means, but depends on feature meaning. For GMM, scaling also
affects covariance estimation. Avoid blindly scaling features without
considering consequences.

What happens in high dimensions? Distances can become less
discriminative and GMM covariance estimation becomes harder as parameter
counts grow. Feature selection, dimensionality reduction and
regularization may help.

How do you assess clustering stability? Repeat fits with different
seeds, bootstrap or subsample data, and compare partitions using metrics
such as ARI when suitable.

What if a K-Means cluster is empty? An empty cluster can occur after
assignment. Implementations handle this with reinitialization or
safeguards. Inspect resulting cluster sizes rather than assuming all
requested clusters are meaningful.

How can clustering help anomaly detection? Points far from centroids or
with low likelihood under a fitted mixture may be unusual. Clustering is
not automatically an anomaly detector; thresholds and evaluation must
fit the task.

What is a K-Means centroid? The arithmetic mean of observations assigned
to a cluster. It may not be an actual observation and can be difficult
to interpret in transformed feature spaces.

Can a GMM assign a point to multiple clusters?

K-Means & GMM | Interview Study Guide Page 5  It can give non-zero
posterior probability to multiple components. A downstream system may
choose the maximum-probability component but can preserve the full
probability vector to represent uncertainty.

8.  Scenario-based and advanced questions You have elongated clusters.
    What would you try? Compare K-Means with a GMM using an appropriate
    covariance structure, such as full covariance. Inspect geometry and
    validate with multiple criteria; full-covariance GMM is not
    guaranteed to be correct.

Your clusters have very different sizes and variances. What would you
consider? GMM may be a stronger candidate because each component can
have its own covariance. Compare covariance types and assess stability
and fit.

Inertia decreases as k increases. Does that prove larger k is better?
No. This is expected. Use elbow, silhouette, stability, domain
usefulness or other suitable criteria to balance fit and complexity.

Silhouette prefers k=2 but BIC prefers k=3. What do you do? Explain that
they measure different things. Inspect geometry, compare stability and
evaluate task usefulness rather than declaring one metric universally
correct.

A GMM returns probabilities 0.36, 0.34 and 0.30. What does that mean?
The model is uncertain about component membership. The first is the most
likely label, but the small probability differences should be preserved
if uncertainty matters.

K-Means changes significantly across seeds. What would you do? Increase
n_init, inspect overlap and assumptions, scale appropriately, assess
stability and consider another algorithm if K-Means geometry is
unsuitable.

A GMM covariance estimate is unstable. What can help? Use reg_covar,
inspect duplicate or collinear features, consider a simpler
covariance_type, scale sensibly and check whether sample size supports
the parameter count.

You have millions of observations. What alternative may help?
MiniBatchKMeans updates centroids from small batches and can scale
better than standard batch K-Means. Validate whether its approximation
is adequate.

A stakeholder asks what Cluster 0 means. What do you say? Cluster IDs
are arbitrary. Profile members using original business features,
identify distinguishing characteristics and assign a descriptive name
only after validating usefulness and stability.

When might you reject clustering? If there is no meaningful group
structure, features do not represent the task, clusters are unstable or
not actionable, or a supervised or rule-based approach better fits the
objective.

How is K-Means used in vector search? K-Means can group embedding
vectors into coarse clusters or centroids. IVF-style indexes use those
groups to narrow the search region instead of comparing every vector.

What is vector quantization? K-Means centroids can form a codebook. A
vector can be represented by the index of its nearest centroid,
supporting compression. Your README connects this idea to VQ-VAE
codebooks.
